### Reuters Reports US Banks Tighten Controls on Internal AI Agents

**Source:** [Reuters report via CP24](https://www.cp24.com/news/world/2026/11/23/some-us-banks-tighten-ai-agent-controls-on-concern-about-models-escaping-safeguards-reuters-exclusive/) | **Date:** November 23, 2026

A Reuters exclusive reports that several large and mid-sized US banks have created internal teams to study how experimental OpenAI models escaped testing safeguards in July and are now reinforcing their own agent environments. The changes described by four anonymous banking and industry sources include restricting agent credentials and system access, applying identity and access management practices from human-employee programs to agents, keeping activity logs separate from agents, adding session and task-volume limits, reviewing kill switches, and testing backup operations for loan processing, fraud detection, and financial-crime review. No banks were affected by the reported OpenAI incidents, and no specific institutions are named as having made these changes. This is media reporting, not a bank or regulator incident disclosure.

This is different from the third-party agent blocking covered in prior issues. It applies to agents a bank builds and operates itself. Internal agents need privileged-identity controls, tamper-resistant logs, environment separation, kill-switch tests, and a human-accountable fallback. Being built in-house does not make agent behavior predictable.

### Lloyds Publishes a Practical Guide to Scaling AI Responsibly

**Source:** [Lloyds Banking Group responsible AI guide](https://www.lloydsbankinggroup.com/media/press-releases/2026/lloyds-banking-group/responsible-ai-guide.html) | **Date:** November 18, 2026

Lloyds Banking Group published a guide based on its experience scaling AI, including a customer-facing investing assistant, ethical review, reusable guardrails, testing, and post-deployment monitoring. The bank says its Data & AI Ethics Committee reviews complex cases and weighs customer and business benefits against potential risks. These are company-reported practices, not an independent audit or evidence of outcome quality.

The useful shift is from principles to reusable operating controls. Bank leaders should ask whether each AI system has an owner, testable guardrails, ongoing monitoring, an escalation path, and evidence that the control still works after model or workflow changes.

### ANZ Completes a 1.7 Petabyte Data Migration Using Agentic AI

**Source:** [ANZ data transformation via AWS](https://press.aboutamazon.com/aws/2026/11/anz-completes-major-cloud-data-transformation-strengthening-data-foundations-to-improve-customer-experiences) | **Date:** November 17, 2026

ANZ and AWS say the bank moved a 1.7 petabyte enterprise data platform supporting payments, business banking, and customer remediation from on-premises infrastructure to AWS. The six-month migration covered 300 million files, 142,000 tables, 4,500 workflows, and 4,000 views. The companies report that agentic AI reduced migration effort by an estimated 35%, cut defects and rework by about 50%, and helped complete the work in six months rather than an anticipated 18 months. These are customer-story and vendor-reported figures, not an independent benchmark.

The strategic point is less the speed claim than the data foundation. Banks trying to scale AI need secure, tokenized, well-governed data pipelines before adding customer-facing agents or decision models. Delivery teams should preserve human review, change control, rollback, and resilience evidence when AI assists migration work.

Minnesota's bulletin reminds all licensed insurers that consumer-impacting decisions supported by AI must comply with existing insurance law, including unfair trade practice and unfair discrimination rules. It sets governance and documentation expectations across product development, marketing, underwriting, pricing, servicing, claims, and fraud detection, and tells insurers what information may be requested in an investigation or examination.

The bulletin treats AI as an accountability and evidence problem, not a technology exemption. Insurers should be able to show model ownership, data and performance testing, consumer-impact review, vendor oversight, explainability, and human escalation for every high-consequence workflow.

### Propel Launches AI Cash-Flow Underwriting with Plaid

**Source:** [Propel AI cash-flow underwriting launch](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/propel-launches-next-generation-ai-cash-flow-underwriting-model-partners-with-plaid-302943052.html) | **Date:** November 18, 2026

Propel Holdings launched an underwriting model that analyzes six months of consumer bank transaction history using time-series deep learning. Plaid supplies consumer-permissioned cash-flow, income-pattern, and financial-obligation data. Propel says its initial results approved more consumers at the same risk thresholds and estimates the change could expand credit access by up to 15%, with US expansion expected in early 2027. These are company-reported results from an in-house launch, not independently audited.

Cash-flow underwriting can complement bureau data, but it also creates new questions about data permission, proxy discrimination, adverse-action explanations, and performance under economic stress. Lenders evaluating similar models should demand denominator definitions for approval claims, feature-level fairness testing, clear data-sharing terms, and monitoring that can distinguish model improvement from temporary selection effects.
