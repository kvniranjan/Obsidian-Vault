### Lloyds Bank Readies AI-Powered Scam Check to Stop Shopping Fraud Before Transfer
**Source:** [FinTech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/06/17/lloyds-new-tool-flags-scams-before-money-leaves/)  •  **Date:** June 17, 2026

Lloyds Bank is preparing to roll out Scam Check, an in-app feature that intercepts online shopping fraud before funds leave the account. When a customer initiates a payment to a new payee for a purchase, the tool prompts them for screenshots and uses AI to scan for red flags including implausibly low prices, newly created seller accounts with no ratings, vague item descriptions, and pressure tactics. If suspicious signals are detected, the customer receives a warning before the transfer completes. Context: 68% of Lloyds fraud reports relate to shopping scams; the majority originate on Meta-owned platforms; the bank blocked over £1 billion in attempted fraud during 2025 and already checks an average of 23,551 transactions per minute.

Intercepting fraud at the point of payment instruction — before money moves — is structurally better than post-payment recovery, especially under the UK's expanded APP reimbursement rules. The interesting design question is false-positive rate: a tool that warns too aggressively will erode customer trust in legitimate purchases, while one that is too permissive misses the goal. Lloyds has committed £100 million to new fraud prevention technology and is also deploying AI to assist fraud colleagues during live customer calls, suggesting a layered rather than single-point approach.

### Visa and Mastercard Build Tokenized Credential Layer for AI Agent Payments
**Source:** [PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2026/visa-and-mastercard-put-tokens-in-charge-of-ai-commerce/)  •  **Date:** June 17, 2026

Visa launched Agent Score, Agentic Directory, and enhanced token capabilities to verify and credentialize autonomous transactions; Mastercard announced Agent Pay integrations with Wizard and Stripe to link AI-driven product discovery directly to tokenized payment execution. Both networks are building around the same principle: rather than transmitting sensitive account details, tokenized credentials let consumers authorize AI agents to transact within predefined limits while protecting underlying payment data from merchants. According to PYMNTS Intelligence's Prompt Economy research, 95% of consumers express concerns about AI-driven purchasing, but 50% would trust agentic commerce if clear fraud protections are in place.

The authorization layer below agentic commerce — where identity, credentials, spend limits, and trust frameworks converge — is becoming one of the most strategically contested surfaces in digital payments. Both networks are positioning themselves as the trust anchor for agent-initiated transactions, which is a significant claim: it means their token infrastructure would govern authentication, revocation, limit enforcement, and dispute resolution for a new class of non-human actors. Banks that issue cards onto these networks should understand what controls they retain over agent-initiated transactions and what their liability exposure looks like when an authorized agent acts outside customer intent.

The 93% false-positive reduction claim is the number compliance teams should probe before purchasing: false-positive rates depend heavily on data quality, rule calibration, and customer base, and vendors do not always disclose the baseline they are comparing against. The governance angle matters more than the headline number. An AI system that autonomously resolves AML alerts without adequate audit trails, explainability records, and escalation controls can create regulatory exposure even if it is operationally efficient. Banks replacing legacy compliance infrastructure with AI need to map each AI-assisted step to an accountable human decision point for examiners.

### EU Digital Omnibus Provisionally Defers High-Risk AI Deadline by 16 Months
**Source:** [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)  •  **Date:** Provisional agreement May 7, 2026; formal adoption expected before August 2, 2026

The EU Digital Omnibus provisional agreement, reached on May 7, 2026, moves the compliance deadline for most high-risk AI systems under Annex III of the EU AI Act from August 2, 2026 to December 2, 2027, a 16-month extension. Financial services systems explicitly classified as high-risk and covered by this postponement include credit scoring and loan approval tools; AI embedded in regulated Annex I products (such as medical devices and machinery) gets a further extension to August 2, 2028. Critically, Article 50 transparency obligations — requiring disclosure when users interact with AI systems — still take effect August 2, 2026, with a four-month grace period for watermarking requirements. The agreement is not yet formally adopted; publication in the Official Journal is expected in the coming weeks, before the August 2 date.

The postponement matters for credit, insurance, and financial-crime AI teams that had been racing toward an August deadline. Sixteen additional months is not a reprieve from the work; it is a longer runway to do it properly. The obligations — risk management documentation, data governance, technical evidence, human oversight records, post-market monitoring, and serious-incident reporting — remain unchanged. Banks that used the August deadline as the forcing function for AI inventory work should not let the extension dissolve that urgency. Formal adoption is still pending, and the AML and fraud-detection teams that were already deep into classification should finish the assessment regardless.
