### US Banks Restrict Third-Party AI Agents, Raising Liability and Access Questions

**Source:** [American Banker — Banks are blocking external AI agents: is this sustainable?](https://www.americanbanker.com/news/banks-are-blocking-external-ai-agents-is-this-sustainable)  •  [American Banker — Banks should reconsider putting AI agent liability on consumers](https://www.americanbanker.com/payments/news/banks-should-reconsider-putting-ai-agent-liability-on-consumers)  •  **Date:** November 23–24, 2026

Major US banks including Bank of America, Citi, JPMorgan, and Wells Fargo have been blocking third-party AI agents from accessing customer accounts and initiating transactions, citing fears of agents acting independently of user intent. Where banks have not blocked agents outright, several have updated their terms of service to shift liability for agent-initiated actions to the customer — Wells Fargo's revised Online Access Agreement effective November 24 treats any agent instruction as directly authorized by the account holder. American Banker analysis argues that this defensive approach protects banks from early-stage fraud but creates a retention risk as tech companies build agent-friendly financial infrastructure without the same restrictions.

The access and liability split is not a final answer: it is an interim risk allocation while identity, authorization, and revocation standards do not yet exist. Banks should distinguish between blocking unauthenticated agents and governing authenticated ones — a blanket restriction that treats every agent as a threat will push agent-enabled commerce toward platforms that have no bank-grade fraud controls at all. The sustainable position is a standards-based access layer with explicit mandates, transaction limits, and audit trails, not an indefinite terms-of-service patch.

### McKinsey "The AI Disruption Dividend": Ten-Month Payback, But Deposit Income Faces Structural Threat

**Source:** [McKinsey & Company — Global Banking Annual Review 2026: The AI Disruption Dividend](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/mckinsey/industries/financial%20services/our%20insights/global%20banking%20annual%20review%202026%20part%202/global-banking-annual-review-2026-the-ai-disruption-dividend.pdf)  •  **Date:** November 10, 2026

McKinsey's latest banking annual review estimates that AI implementations recover their investment in roughly ten months, with a three-year ROI of approximately 2.9x. The report finds that nearly one quarter of retail banking customers across 13 surveyed markets already use generative AI for financial tasks, yet only about one quarter of companies deploying generative AI have redesigned any processes around it. The report also flags agentic AI as a structural threat to traditional deposit income: agents that optimize balances in real time could move material sums from low-yield checking accounts into higher-yield alternatives, compressing the spread banks have historically counted on.

The two findings are in tension. Banks are demonstrating short-term operational ROI from AI automation while simultaneously underinvesting in the process redesign and governance infrastructure needed to capture it at scale. The deposit disruption scenario is not hypothetical — it is a direct consequence of agent capabilities already in market. Banks should model the balance migration risk in parallel with AI deployment plans and stress-test treasury assumptions against an environment where retail customers have always-on optimization running on their behalf.

### Industry Launches "Know-Your-Agent" Pilots to Authenticate AI Payment Actors

**Source:** [Global Finance Magazine — Know-Your-Agent Testing Targets Agentic Payment Fraud](https://gfmag.com/technology/know-your-agent-pilots-target-agentic-payment-fraud/)  •  **Date:** November 20, 2026

Two separate industry pilots are testing frameworks to verify an AI agent's identity and transaction authority before a payment clears. Visa, BILL, and Billtrust ran a live pilot in which AI agents initiate and complete approved buyer invoice payments using Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol; the protocol authenticates the agent before BILL processes the payment. Separately, Kinexys (J.P. Morgan's blockchain unit) completed an initial pilot with Hyundai Card in which a treasury agent operated under a digitally signed AI agent mandate, managing euro liquidity in a Kinexys Blockchain Deposit Account in Frankfurt with the ability to request FX executions — Kinexys could auto-execute, escalate to a human, or deny each request depending on whether it matched the mandate. The article notes that anti-fraud systems calibrated to flag unusual behavioral patterns will generate false declines for legitimate agent activity unless agent identity and authority can be verified upstream.

The underlying problem is that fraud controls and payment rails were built for human actors with stable behavioral signatures. Agent-initiated payments are machine-speed, mandate-scoped, and potentially high-volume, which looks like enumeration or account takeover to current detection systems. The pilots show two viable verification approaches — protocol-layer attestation and signed mandates — but neither is yet interoperable or at scale. Banks, card networks, and payment processors should begin mapping which fraud logic is incompatible with legitimate agent traffic and which infrastructure changes are required before agent payment volume matters.

### AI Cannot Fix What Fragmented Compliance Data Breaks

**Source:** [FinTech Global — Can compliance systems actually talk to each other?](https://fintech.global/2026/11/27/can-compliance-systems-actually-talk-to-each-other/)  •  **Date:** November 27, 2026

Part two of FinTech Global's RegTech Analyst Series finds that most financial institutions have layered compliance tools one regulation at a time, leaving each system with its own version of the client: screening holds name and date of birth, onboarding holds documents, the risk engine holds a score, and case management holds analyst notes. The article cites a Deloitte 2025 figure that only one quarter of financial-services respondents rated their data platforms as highly prepared for generative AI. RegTech CEOs quoted in the piece warn that ten systems can require up to 45 point-to-point connections, each of which breaks when either side changes, and that the deeper issue is not connectivity but meaning — different systems use the same field labels for different things. The critical warning: "An agent working from five inconsistent sources will produce a confident answer from the wrong one."

The article recommends that the firm — not the vendor — own the map between regulatory obligations, internal policies, and controls, and that AI suggestions be kept clearly distinct from approved interpretations. The practical starting point it identifies is a single complete compliance workflow with stable identifiers, clear data definitions, and usable APIs. Banks investing in AI-assisted compliance and financial-crime tooling should run this diagnostic first; automating on a fragmented foundation does not make the fragmentation invisible, it makes it consequential and fast.

### Insurance AI Adoption Reaches 62% of Firms; Sector Resists AI-Specific Regulation

**Source:** [FinTech Global — Does insurance need a specific regulation for AI?](https://fintech.global/2026/11/26/does-insurance-need-a-specific-regulation-for-ai/)  •  **Date:** November 26, 2026

A Goldman Sachs survey of 434 insurance CIOs and CFOs found that 62% of firms now use AI in operations, up from 48% in 2025 and 29% in 2024. EIOPA data cited in the article shows 40% of insurers currently use generative AI in customer service, with 48% more planning to adopt it, and 32% using it in claims management with 59% more planning to do so. An Earnix 2026 report finds 81% of respondents have AI integrated across some or most functions, while 83% worry their models were trained on incomplete or inaccurate data. Industry experts quoted in the article argue against a bespoke AI regulatory regime, favouring instead clearer guidance under existing consumer protection, fairness, and accountability duties such as the UK FCA's Consumer Duty; they identify blackbox models, data quality, and third-party vendor oversight as the areas that most need addressing.

The same dynamics apply to banking credit and pricing. Where insurers worry about unexplainable underwriting decisions and biased outcomes from vendor-supplied models, bank credit risk and product-pricing teams face structurally identical questions. The article's implicit guidance — that explainability and data-quality controls are the substance of compliance, whatever the form of the rule — is more immediately actionable than waiting for AI-specific sectoral legislation that may not arrive.

### AI Governance for Compliance Crosses Into Operations as 2027 Approaches

**Source:** [FinTech Global — AI governance becomes a must-have for compliance in 2027](https://fintech.global/2026/11/25/ai-governance-becomes-a-must-have-for-compliance-in-2027/)  •  **Date:** November 25, 2026

RegTech firm Saifr identifies five shifts underway as AI moves from experimental to operational in compliance functions. First, embedded intelligence is replacing standalone tools — AI is being built into the compliance workflow rather than applied as a separate filter. Second, context per alert is replacing alert volume as the primary performance metric. Third, AI governance is shifting from best practice to business necessity as regulators apply existing obligations (the EU AI Act and existing national rules) to AI-generated decisions, leaving firms accountable for outcomes whether a person or a machine made the call. Fourth, AML and financial-crime teams are investing in signal intelligence — identifying which inputs most reliably predict enforcement action — rather than simply increasing alert throughput. Fifth, purpose-built, smaller models are outperforming larger general-purpose models on specialised compliance tasks, with advantages in cost, explainability, and governance.

The governance-as-necessity shift is the most material for compliance officers. It means that AI system inventories, oversight structures, vendor contracts, human review workflows, and documentation requirements are now regulatory infrastructure, not optional maturity work. Banks that have treated AI governance as a later-stage initiative should accelerate it: regulators are not waiting for a separate AI compliance rule, they are applying what exists now.
