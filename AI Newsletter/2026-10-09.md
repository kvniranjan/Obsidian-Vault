Banks that have not stress-tested the deposit-cost impact of a 10% AI-power-user cohort growing to 20–30% should run those scenarios now; the liability side of the balance sheet is the underappreciated AI risk.

As banks and insurers accelerate agentic deployment, the security gap is less about agent identity (who the agent is) and more about agent scope (what the agent actually does once authorized). An enterprise onboarding agent that strays beyond its assigned task is Rein's reference case — and a category of failure that existing fraud and identity controls are not designed to detect.

Under the model, users verify once through Sumsub; regulated partners (on/off-ramps, card issuers, payment providers) receive verified data with user consent instead of running independent checks from scratch. For BFSI compliance teams, reusable KYC at this scale represents infrastructure change, not workflow improvement. The friction point in embedded finance and cross-border payment onboarding is not verification difficulty — it's redundancy: the same customer completing separate KYC flows for every regulated partner in the same network. Sumsub's portable credential model eliminates that redundancy while preserving each partner's ability to impose additional checks for higher-risk transactions. The 25-point lift in conversion is a credible commercial lever that compliance-led KYC redesigns often struggle to quantify, making it useful evidence in internal debates about budget for digital identity infrastructure.

Institutions benchmarking AI ROI primarily against false-positive rates and headcount reduction should ask whether they are measuring the floor. The laggard pattern — strong on detection, weak on growth impact — maps directly onto organizations that started with AML alert optimization and stopped there.

### Who Is Responsible When the AI Adviser Gets It Wrong?
**Source:** [FinTech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/10/07/who-is-responsible-when-the-ai-adviser-gets-it-wrong/)  •  **Date:** October 7, 2026

As wealth managers deploy agentic AI to scale personalized advice, a new analysis with two industry experts concludes that the financial firm — not the model provider — bears accountability under current frameworks, and that the governance gap lies in firms adopting tools they cannot audit or explain. An Opinium survey of 2,000 UK adults found 54% have used AI to support investment decisions, but trust is nearly evenly split: 45% trust the technology, 49% do not. Fredrik Davéus (Kidbrooke) argues MiFID II is largely technology-neutral and adequate: firms are failing not because rules are wrong but because they deploy systems that cannot satisfy existing suitability and audit obligations. Friedhelm Schmitt (Fincite) takes the harder line, calling for regulation to become machine-readable so AI systems can apply and prove compliance continuously. Both agree that clients should be able to identify when AI played a material role and be able to opt out.

With M1 launching an SEC-fiduciary AI adviser and HSBC cutting up to 70% of UK wealth advisers (both reported earlier this week), the accountability question is no longer theoretical. The Kidbrooke governance framework is practically actionable: separate the financial-logic reasoning engine (suitability, portfolio construction, audit trail, version control) from the language interface (which only explains outcomes), and build explicit escalation paths. The 54% UK adoption rate with near-even trust suggests the deployment window is open — but firms unable to explain how AI-generated advice was produced face MiFID II suitability exposure before the EU AI Act's high-risk provisions take full effect.
