### NAIC Adopts an AI Examination Supplement for Insurers

**Source:** [NAIC Fall National Meeting summary](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/national_meeting/summary-big-data-ai-wg111626-final.pdf) | **Date:** November 16, 2026

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners' Big Data and Artificial Intelligence Working Group adopted the AI Risk Evaluation Supplement after a 12-state pilot, public exposure, and two technical edits. The supplement gives state regulators a risk-based way to ask about an insurer's AI inventory, governance program, high-risk systems, data, human oversight, and third-party models. The published resource says it supplements existing examination procedures and does not independently create substantive legal duties, standards of conduct, or expanded regulatory authority. This is a regulator examination resource, not a new insurance law.

This makes AI evidence an examination-readiness problem, not just a model-governance project. Insurers should be able to identify which systems have direct consumer impact or material financial impact, who owns them, what data and vendors they use, how changes trigger revalidation, and whether human reviewers actually reverse or escalate decisions. A nominal human-in-the-loop control is weak if the reviewer lacks authority, qualifications, capacity, or an auditable record of intervention.

### Visa Extends Agentic Ready to Merchants and Acquirers

**Source:** [Visa Agentic Ready expansion](https://corporate.visa.com/content/VISA/visacorporate/global/en/home/sites/visa-perspectives/newsroom/visa-agentic-ready-expansion.html) | **Date:** November 17, 2026

Visa expanded its Agentic Ready program beyond issuers to merchants, payment service providers, acquirers, B2B suppliers, and commerce platforms. Visa says the acceptance phase launched with more than 140 participants, while more than 175 issuers across 45 markets were already in the program. Partners can test how agent-initiated transactions are recognized, authenticated, processed, and supported through access, discovery and cart, payment, and post-purchase stages. These are Visa-reported program participation and capability claims, not independent evidence of live fraud, dispute, or conversion performance.

The important shift is that agentic commerce is now being designed at the acceptance edge, where merchants must decide whether to expose inventory, accept a delegated mandate, authorize the payment, and resolve a return or dispute. Merchants and acquirers should not treat an agent label as sufficient. They need a verifiable principal, a bounded purpose, a payment record that preserves the mandate, and post-purchase controls that still work when no human was present at checkout.

### PNC Sets a Formal Boundary for Third-Party AI Agents

**Source:** [PNC Digital Services Agreement](https://www.pnc.com/content/dam/digital-assets/en/documents/digital_services_agreement_UPDATE.pdf) | **Date:** Effective December 19, 2026

PNC's posted Digital Services Agreement defines a third-party AI agent and describes a controlled connection model in which the customer authenticates directly with PNC and specifies the account, information, duration, and permissions. Unless PNC expressly allows it for a particular service, the agreement prohibits an agent from initiating, requesting, submitting, or completing a payment, transfer, or other transaction. It also prohibits sharing credentials with an agent and using browser automation, screen scraping, or similar methods to bypass PNC's connection methods. PNC reserves the right to block, restrict, delay, decline, or require additional authentication for agent activity. The agreement is future-effective, so it is not evidence that all of these capabilities are live today.

This is a concrete contract boundary around the vague phrase "AI agent access." Banks should distinguish read access, preparation, transaction initiation, and transaction execution instead of treating them as one permission. They also need to align customer disclosures, error handling, applicable law, and account-specific liability terms. A broad disclaimer cannot replace a sound authorization and fraud-control design.

### ThreatFabric Launches Know Your Agent Controls for Banking Sessions

**Source:** [ThreatFabric Agentic AI Risk and Know Your Agent](https://www.threatfabric.com/press-releases/agentic-ai-risk) | **Date:** November 19, 2026

ThreatFabric launched Agentic AI Risk and Know Your Agent capabilities inside its Fraud Risk Suite. The vendor says the system combines a new behavioral AI model with device risk and mobile threat intelligence across onboarding, login, navigation, and payment. It is designed to distinguish legitimate agents from malicious, hijacked, or fraudster-controlled ones across 22 agentic fraud use cases, including accessibility abuse, cloud-hosted agents, victim coaching, agentic account takeover, and malicious intent. ThreatFabric reports that every agent-generated payment was identified as "not the customer" in a reference deployment. That result is vendor-reported, with no independent benchmark or false-positive distribution disclosed.

Detecting automation is not the same as deciding whether the automation is authorized. Banks should test legitimate assistants, accessibility tools, remote-support software, agent impersonation, and compromised devices against the same controls. The evidence should show who the agent represents, what mandate applies, what it attempted, and why the bank allowed, challenged, or stopped the action.

### Allianz Says AI Insurance Losses May Be Hidden in Existing Categories

**Source:** [Allianz Commercial report on AI-related claims](https://commercial.allianz.com/news-and-insights/news/artificial-intelligence-claims-2026.html) | **Date:** November 19, 2026

Allianz Commercial said its analysis of AI risk shows confirmed AI losses understating the broader exposure because claims can be recorded under cyber, directors and officers, product liability, or crime policies. The insurer says critical AI incidents increased by more than 150% over the last two years and that plausible loss scenarios can reach the triple-digit millions of U.S. dollars. It proposes four categories: direct AI loss, AI-related loss, AI-accelerated loss, and AI-dependency loss. Allianz says it is already tracking AI-related claims with values in the millions of euros, but the figures are insurer analysis and scenario estimates, not an independent industry loss database.

The practical problem is attribution. If an AI error causes a bad financial recommendation, a cyber incident, a misleading disclosure, or a prolonged outage after a fallback fails, multiple policies and exclusions may collide. Insurers should build a common AI-loss taxonomy, capture model and vendor involvement in claims files, test aggregation and systemic-risk assumptions, and make fallback obligations explicit. Customers should not assume that a policy written before widespread AI use will answer these questions cleanly.
