### A7 Sanctions Scandal Exposes Fatal Gaps in Periodic Compliance Checks

**Source:** [Fintech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/09/24/a7-scandal-exposes-gaps-in-periodic-compliance-checks/)  •  **Date:** September 24, 2026

A Financial Times investigation revealed that Kremlin-backed fintech A7 funneled over $6.9 billion through the international banking system despite existing sanctions on Russia, using front companies, fabricated invoices, correspondent banking access, and SWIFT to move illicit funds globally. The National Crime Agency suggests that figure represents only partial visibility; the network allegedly settled more than $86 billion in transactions in its first year alone. The case exposes the core weakness of annual or periodic compliance reviews: a customer who was low-risk at onboarding is not necessarily low-risk twelve months later, and over 52% of firms still struggle to validate beneficial ownership in complex structures.

The operational implication is sharp. Sanctions evasion has evolved into state-sponsored alternative payment infrastructure—a threat that rules-only, batch-review systems cannot track in real time. Compliance and BSA/AML leaders should map which customer segments receive only periodic reviews rather than continuous monitoring, assess whether their beneficial-ownership data is current and independently verifiable, and test whether transaction-monitoring logic is calibrated for multi-entity layering schemes rather than single-account anomaly detection.

### Baselayer Lands $35M to Build the "Know Your Agent" Trust Layer

**Source:** [Fintech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/09/23/baselayer-lands-35m-to-verify-ai-agents-before-fraud-hits/)  •  **Date:** September 23, 2026

Baselayer, which already provides identity and risk infrastructure to one in five US financial institutions, raised a $35 million Series A led by M13 and launched its Agentic Identity Suite: the first interoperable trust layer and agentic fraud consortium designed specifically for autonomous AI commerce. The core problem it addresses is one traditional KYC systems cannot solve—"agents don't carry ID, and the infrastructure built to verify humans and businesses simply doesn't recognize them." Before permitting an agent transaction, institutions need to confirm three things: which entity the agent represents, whether it holds authorization to act, and whether the counterparty is trustworthy. Context for the urgency: Stripe reports that AI agents now generate 70% of API data-pull commands, and major networks including Visa, Mastercard, and American Express have already released agent commerce protocols.

This is infrastructure-layer work, not a product feature—Baselayer is attempting to build the plumbing that lets existing fraud and compliance stacks recognize non-human actors at all. Financial institutions deploying or accepting agent-initiated transactions should assess whether their current identity architecture has a concept of "agent" at all before deciding whether to integrate with emerging trust consortia.

### Outerlimit Raises $16M to Apply Zero Trust Principles to AI Agents

**Source:** [Fintech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/09/24/outerlimit-raises-16m-as-agentic-ai-outpaces-security/)  •  **Date:** September 24, 2026

Outerlimit emerged from stealth with $16 million in pre-seed funding to address the authorization gap that enterprise AI agent deployments expose. The company applies Zero Trust security at the individual agent action level: security keys are fragmented across environments and decrypted only after the system confirms identity, policy, and execution context—meaning each agent action is cleared before it runs rather than trusting agent sessions wholesale. The problem is structural: traditional identity and access management tools were built for human users, not for software actors that may access dozens of systems per task, run continuously, and acquire privileges over time.

For bank security teams, the practical concern is that blocking all agent deployment is not a viable long-term stance—it drives AI use into unsanctioned channels—while granting agents broad access creates governance gaps that regulators and auditors are increasingly likely to probe. Outerlimit's per-action enforcement model offers a middle path, though financial institutions evaluating it should verify that the enforcement layer itself is auditable, that key fragmentation does not create new availability risks, and that the system handles multi-agent orchestration chains rather than only single-hop deployments.

### Greenboard Builds AI-Native SEC and FINRA Compliance for 500+ Financial Firms

**Source:** [Fintech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/09/24/how-greenboard-is-building-the-next-generation-of-compliance/)  •  **Date:** September 24, 2026

Greenboard, founded in 2023, positions itself as an AI-native "system of action" for broker-dealer and investment adviser compliance—unifying email review, trade surveillance, brokerage statement processing, and risk assessment into a single platform that serves over 500 financial institutions. A notable differentiator is its prediction market surveillance: Greenboard integrates with Kalshi and Polymarket accounts to monitor employee activity on prediction markets, a risk area where regulatory frameworks remain unsettled but insider trading exposure is real. The AI layer, called Greenboard Co, lets compliance officers query their full compliance stack in natural language, filter false positives before human review, and iterate on forms and risk assessments through conversational prompts—while retaining human control over subjective decisions.

The philosophical bet here—AI handling the tedious while humans retain agency over judgment calls—is increasingly common positioning, but Greenboard's attention to prediction markets is notable. As these venues grow in relevance and asset managers expand into non-traditional instruments, compliance coverage that lags venue adoption creates regulatory exposure. Institutions evaluating similar tools should test classification accuracy on their specific security and instrument mix, confirm auditability of AI decisions for examiners, and verify that the system handles their jurisdiction's specific SEC and FINRA reporting requirements.

### Finosec Launches AI Governance Module to Track Hidden AI in Bank Vendor Products

**Source:** [Finosec / GlobalFintechSeries](https://globalfintechseries.com/banking/finosec-announces-ai-governance-for-banks-and-credit-unions/)  •  **Date:** September 22, 2026

Finosec, a cybersecurity governance platform serving community banks and credit unions, announced an AI Governance module that will be generally available January 1, 2027, with early access for existing customers sooner. The module addresses an exposure that has arrived at thousands of small institutions without a purchase decision: vendors are adding AI capabilities to software banks already own, frequently without a formal announcement or an updated due diligence packet. The product builds a centralized AI inventory from the institution's existing vendor system map, scores each system's risk level, records controls, generates AI-specific due diligence questionnaires for vendors, and produces board-level reporting—all from data the institution already maintains.

This addresses an operational reality for community banks and credit unions: teams are small, AI is arriving through back doors in existing contracts, and examiners are now asking about AI governance using frameworks like the CSBS supervisory guide released in September. The product doesn't require institutions to have already inventoried their AI exposure—it builds the inventory—which matters most for the segment of the market that has been too stretched to do it manually.
