### Six Major Banks Publish Principles to Govern AI Shopping Agents

**Source:** [Reuters via Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/banks-warn-ai-shopping-bots-raise-scam-fraud-and-dataprivacy-risks-4910257)  •  **Date:** September 22, 2026

NatWest, Bank of America, ING, Capital One, ASB Bank, and Commonwealth Bank of Australia jointly released a report laying out principles for agentic commerce—transactions in which AI tools select and purchase goods autonomously on behalf of consumers—and said they intend to bring a set of proposals to policymakers. The banks identified three core risk categories: AI agents requesting card details directly and entering them into merchant sites (bypassing tokenization and fraud controls built into hosted payment pages), agents steering consumers toward payment methods that carry weaker liability protections, and data-privacy exposure when agents persist access to payment credentials beyond a single session. The proposed principles include mandatory disclosure when an AI agent is involved in a transaction, transparency requirements on how agents make decisions, and enforceable safeguards over how customer payment data is retained and shared.

This is the first coordinated cross-bank public statement on agentic commerce risk, and it arrives at the same moment that OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta are each accelerating AI shopping capabilities. The proposals the banks plan to bring to regulators—particularly agent-disclosure requirements and liability assignment when agents transact without explicit per-purchase authorization—would, if adopted, require material changes to how card networks, digital wallets, and AI shopping platforms currently operate. Banks with consumer card businesses, payment-product teams, and customer-facing AI features should treat this report as a preview of the regulatory discussion to come and assess whether their current agent-payment architectures can support the disclosure and audit-trail requirements the signatories are advocating.

### Baselayer Raises $35M to Build Identity Infrastructure for AI Agents

**Source:** [Fintech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/09/23/baselayer-lands-35m-to-verify-ai-agents-before-fraud-hits/)  •  **Date:** September 23, 2026

Baselayer closed a $35 million Series A led by M13, with participation from Torch Capital, Picus Ventures, Afore Capital, and Socure's Matt Thompson, to build identity verification and risk infrastructure specifically for autonomous AI agents transacting in financial systems. CEO Jonathan Awad framed the gap succinctly: financial systems were designed to authenticate humans and registered companies, not software agents. The need is concrete—Stripe has reported that AI agents now generate 70% of API data-pull commands across its platform. Baselayer already serves one in five US financial institutions and says its clients have prevented over $1 billion in fraud losses. Visa, Mastercard, and American Express have each released agent commerce protocols in 2026; Shopify activated agentic sales by default for approximately one million merchants. Baselayer's approach centers on cryptographic identity verification that establishes which entity an agent represents, whether it has authorized scope to act, and whether the counterparty is trustworthy—resolved before a transaction is submitted rather than retrospectively.

The funding lands 24 hours after the six-bank coalition published its agent-commerce principles, illustrating how quickly the investment community is pricing in the "know your agent" compliance gap. For fraud, payments, and technology teams, the practical questions concern how Baselayer's identity layer integrates with existing transaction monitoring and authorization flows, whether the cryptographic anchors it establishes are durable across agent updates or model changes, and how the identity assertions it issues can be reconciled with card-network chargeback and dispute rules that currently assign liability based on human-originated payment flows. As agentic commerce accelerates from pilot to default, agent identity infrastructure may become a prerequisite for card-network acceptance rules rather than an optional vendor service.

### AI-Enabled Banking Fraud Projected to Surpass 2.2 Billion Transactions by 2031

**Source:** [Juniper Research via GlobeNewsWire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/09/21/3365143/0/en/banking-fraud-to-reach-2-2-billion-transactions-by-2031-fuelled-by-growing-ai-enabled-attacks.html)  •  **Date:** September 21, 2026

A new Juniper Research report projects that fraudulent transactions in digital banking and money transfer will grow from 773.7 million in 2025 to 2.2 billion globally by 2031—an increase exceeding 180%. The growth is driven by AI dramatically reducing the cost of producing personalized social engineering attacks at scale, enabling criminals to build synthetic identities that combine authentic data with fabricated elements in ways that are both scalable and hard for existing detection models to flag. The report singles out agentic AI as an emerging threat vector: multi-stage, coordinated attacks that adjust dynamically as victims or fraud controls respond, exploiting the compressed detection windows that instant payment rails create. The recommended defense posture is continuous risk monitoring that combines behavioral, identity, and payment signals across the customer journey before authorization—rather than relying primarily on post-payment controls.

The scale of projected growth—nearly three times 2025 levels within six years—underscores that AI is changing the economics of fraud as fundamentally on the attack side as on the defense side. Fraud and financial crime teams should stress-test whether their current behavioral analytics models are calibrated on transaction data that predates the current generation of AI-generated synthetic identities, verify that their instant-payment monitoring can flag anomalies within the sub-second authorization windows of real-time rails, and assess how their AML typologies would classify the multi-step coordinated patterns that agentic fraud introduces. The report's pre-authorization emphasis also challenges a common program architecture where primary detection logic sits on transaction review queues rather than at point-of-entry.

### Banks Want AI Trade Surveillance But Data Quality Is the Real Barrier

**Source:** [Fintech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/09/21/banks-want-ai-surveillance-but-lack-the-data-to-run-it/)  •  **Date:** September 21, 2026

The 1LoD 2026 Surveillance Benchmarking Survey found a stark adoption gap in AI-driven trade surveillance: 89% of banks want AI-enhanced surveillance, but only 11% have it; 78% want generative AI analysts, but only 7% have deployed them; no bank reported having AI fully embedded in either trade or holistic surveillance. Contrary to expectations, budget and regulation are not the primary blockers—78% of respondents report adequate funding, and 67% say regulatory risk no longer constrains their approach. The actual bottleneck is upstream data infrastructure: 71% of challenges trace to data quality issues including fragmented silos (24%), lack of standardized formats (24%), and poor data consistency (23%), with legacy systems compounding problems for four in five banks. False positives present a related challenge, with 93% of banks experiencing them and 52% rating them a high-priority problem. A further 37% struggle specifically with validating NLP and large language model systems, which the FCA has emphasized must enhance rather than replace human oversight.

The finding that governance and budget are largely resolved—but data is not—inverts the common narrative that banks are blocked by regulatory uncertainty or cost. It suggests that banks which have been treating AI surveillance as primarily a procurement or technology decision need to reframe it as a data infrastructure program: standardizing source feeds, resolving legacy system gaps, and building the metadata and lineage layer that AI models require before they can generate reliable surveillance signals. The false-positive rate is a compounding risk: if AI-generated alerts flood review queues with low-quality flags, the net effect is analyst burnout and degraded coverage rather than the enhanced oversight that regulators expect. Compliance and data teams building business cases for AI surveillance should audit data quality first, then size the AI program against what the infrastructure can actually support.

### Evergreen.ai Launches Free AI Financial Adviser to Reach Unadvised Americans

**Source:** [Fintech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/09/22/evergreen-ai-launches-free-ai-financial-adviser/)  •  **Date:** September 22, 2026

Evergreen.ai, founded by PayPal and Intuit veteran Bill Harris and CTO Bryan Godwin, launched a free AI-powered financial guidance platform targeting the 73% of American adults who do not use a financial adviser. The platform operates as a service of Evergreen Wealth Advisors, a registered investment adviser, and combines generative AI with deterministic calculation engines for retirement projections, cash flow analysis, equity compensation planning (options, RSUs, QSBS), mortgage modeling, tax loss harvesting, college savings, and 401(k) planning, with account aggregation through Plaid. It will be free to users who register during the current beta period through January 1, 2028. The company explicitly distinguishes its approach from general-purpose chatbots by using specialized financial models anchored to verified calculations, and states that user data is encrypted, not sold to third parties, and excluded from public AI model training.

The product's positioning as a registered investment adviser running a free general-access tool sets up a regulatory and business-model question that wealth incumbents should track: if comprehensive AI advice at no consumer cost becomes the default entry point for self-directed financial guidance, it changes the economics of the mass-market advisory segment. The RIA wrapper addresses some regulatory concerns, but the SEC's recent guidance on AI-generated investment recommendations and the challenge of applying suitability and conflict-of-interest standards to algorithm-selected outputs at scale means the compliance architecture underlying this type of platform will receive continued scrutiny. For traditional wealth managers, the more immediate question is whether Plaid-aggregated data flowing into a competing AI platform affects client retention, particularly for customers who are currently inactive or underserved by their primary institution.
