Hong Kong's Securities and Futures Commission issued a formal circular on June 2, 2026, warning licensed firms of a sharp increase in AI-enabled cyber incidents. The SFC cited a 27% rise in cyber incidents in 2025 — up to 15,877 from 12,536 the prior year — and noted that AI tools are lowering technical barriers for attackers, enabling faster exploitation of system vulnerabilities and automated large-scale phishing and social engineering. The circular specifically targeted internet brokers and virtual asset trading platforms and required firms to strengthen patch and vulnerability management, threat detection, and incident response. Senior management is expected to bear "primary accountability" for protecting client assets rather than delegating to IT. The guidance aligns with similar circulars from Australia's financial watchdog in April 2026 and Japan's banking authority in May 2026.

Three major financial regulators in the Asia-Pacific region have now issued AI cyber warnings within a three-month window — Australia, Japan, and now Hong Kong. This is a pattern, not a coincidence. Firms operating across these markets should review whether their cyber risk governance frameworks treat AI-assisted threats as a named category with specific controls and board reporting, and whether senior accountability is documented at a level that would survive a supervisory review. The emerging regulatory consensus is clear: AI-powered cyber risk is a senior management responsibility.

### Fiserv Deploys Cognition's Devin AI Engineer for Core Banking Modernisation
**Source:** [Fiserv / GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/05/28/3302992/0/en/Fiserv-and-Cognition-Partner-to-Modernize-Banking-Technology-and-Bring-New-Capabilities-to-Clients-Faster.html) / [Finovate](https://finovate.com/fiserv-turns-to-devin-ai-to-speed-core-banking-upgrades/)  •  **Date:** May 28, 2026

Fiserv announced a strategic partnership with Cognition to deploy Devin, Cognition's autonomous AI software engineer, across Fiserv's core platform modernisation initiatives. Devin can autonomously plan, write, test, and iterate across large, complex codebases — making it particularly suited for legacy banking systems where the codebase is extensive and modernisation has historically been slow. Fiserv said the deployment includes strengthened governance and security controls for AI-assisted development and is designed to accelerate new capability delivery to Fiserv's financial institution clients. The company framed modernisation as "among the most significant and historically slowest initiatives in financial services" and positioned Devin as the way to change that pace.

Using an autonomous coding agent on core banking infrastructure is a different risk profile than using AI for customer-facing applications — errors in a core platform affect transactions, settlements, regulatory reporting, and reconciliation simultaneously. Fiserv's inclusion of governance controls for the software development lifecycle is the right instinct. Banks that license Fiserv core systems should ask about the testing, review, and rollback procedures attached to AI-assisted code changes before those changes reach production. Speed of delivery is valuable; undetected bugs in core banking are not.

Nacha's ACH Fraud Monitoring Rule Phase 2 becomes effective June 19, 2026 — with June 22 as the practical first banking day due to the federal holiday — extending mandatory fraud monitoring requirements to all remaining ACH originators, Third-Party Service Providers, and Third-Party Senders regardless of transaction volume. Phase 1 covered high-volume originators; Phase 2 closes the gap by requiring every ACH participant to establish and implement risk-based fraud detection processes, documented and subject to annual review. Compliance programs must include anomaly detection, velocity checks, and beneficiary-change controls, with automation generally required to operate at the speed and volume of modern ACH rails.

This is an operational compliance deadline that falls inside the next two weeks. Smaller originators and their TPSPs who assumed Phase 2 was still distant need to confirm their fraud monitoring programs are documented and reviewable today. The rule does not specify a particular technical approach, but firms using AI-based anomaly scoring should ensure the system is configured to meet the risk-based standard, outputs are logged, and the program can be demonstrated to Nacha or an examiner. Last-minute exception: any organisation that prepared for Phase 1 and assumed Phase 2 would be delayed should verify that assumption against Nacha's current compliance calendar.
