### APRA Treats AI as a System Risk, Not Just a Model Risk

**Source:** [APRA System Risk Outlook - November 2026](https://www.apra.gov.au/news-and-publications/apras-system-risk-outlook-november-2026) | **Date:** November 12, 2026

Australia's prudential regulator identified four ways AI can affect financial stability: use inside institutions, use against institutions, shared AI dependencies, and broader economic and financial-system changes. APRA said frontier AI is increasing the speed and scale of cyber threats, while reliance on common models, platforms, and service providers can create channels for widespread disruption. It plans to examine AI-driven structural change through the 2027 System Stress Test across banks, superannuation funds, life insurers, and the wider financial system. This is regulator analysis and supervisory direction, not a forecast of a specific failure.

The useful shift is from model inventory to dependency mapping. Banks and insurers should identify which providers, models, data pipelines, and cloud services are common across critical processes, then test what happens when a model is wrong, unavailable, compromised, or changed without notice. A strong AI plan that cannot explain concentration, substitution, recovery, and manual fallback risk is incomplete.

### OCC Gives Examiners a Consequence-Based AI Supervisory Lens

**Source:** [OCC Super Intelligence: Supervisory Framework](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2026/bulletin-2026-54.html) | **Date:** November 4, 2026

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency issued a supervisory framework to help examiners evaluate banks' use of what it calls super intelligence in normal-course activities. The guide does not establish new supervisory expectations for banks, but it tells examiners to tailor reviews to a bank's size, complexity, risk profile, and the materiality of each use case. It emphasizes that control intensity should rise with the potential consequences of an agent's failure or misuse, and that risk classification can depend on consequences, transferred agency, and human oversight rather than technical sophistication.

That is a practical test for banks moving from copilots to agents. Map every use case by what it can change, who bears the loss when it fails, and how quickly a human can intervene. A high-performing model with broad authority is a higher-control deployment than a weaker model that only drafts text. Boards and exam teams should see the action log, approval boundary, rollback path, and evidence of testing, not just a model card.

### Feedzai Adds Agent Detection to the Bank's Front Door

**Source:** [Feedzai via PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/your-next-bank-customer-might-not-be-human-feedzai-helps-banks-spot-ai-agents-at-the-front-door-302937139.html) | **Date:** November 12, 2026

Feedzai expanded its Digital Trust solution to help banks detect AI agents accessing customer accounts. The vendor says the update combines persistent device fingerprinting, network signals, a fraud score based on shared intelligence, and cryptographic device binding linking the customer, device, and bank. Feedzai reports 44% more fraud detected in the riskiest 0.1% of sessions with no increase in false positives, 6,000 fewer unnecessary authentication challenges per million logins versus competing technology, and 41% fewer SMS one-time passcodes. These are vendor-reported metrics.

The hard problem is classification, not detection. A bank must distinguish an agent the customer intentionally authorized from one using stolen credentials or operating beyond the customer's intent. Institutions should define agent allow-list rules, consent evidence, step-up triggers, customer recovery when a legitimate agent is blocked, and the records needed to resolve a later dispute. Blocking every agent is not a viable strategy if the bank wants to remain in the transaction path.

### Kakao Pay Securities Connects Fraud, AML, and Investor Protection

**Source:** [Seoul Economic Daily](https://en.sedaily.com/market/2026/11/12/kakao-pay-securities-rolls-out-ai-fraud-detection-system) | **Date:** November 12, 2026

Kakao Pay Securities said it has put an AI-based fraud detection system into live transactions. The system covers deposits, withdrawals, account openings, stock sales, currency exchange, share transfers to other brokerages, and user-information changes. It combines transaction history, access-environment signals, fund flows, and links across transactions to identify account takeover, suspicious asset sales, abnormal movements, illegal gambling, and illegal private lending. When compound risk signals appear, the system gives staff a risk score and reasoning for review. The deployment and capabilities are company-reported; no accuracy rate was disclosed.

This is a better operating pattern than treating fraud and AML as separate queues. Brokerage fraud often moves across cash, securities, currency, and access controls before a withdrawal occurs. Financial institutions building similar systems should measure false rejects by transaction type, preserve the evidence behind each alert, and connect detection to customer verification, notification, remediation, and formal AML escalation rather than stopping at a score.

### Marsh Pushes Credit Insurance Into the AI Lending Concentration Debate

**Source:** [Insurance Journal, reporting Bloomberg](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2026/11/12/892334.htm) | **Date:** November 12, 2026

Insurance Journal reported that Marsh is urging US regulators to let banks receive capital relief for loans covered by credit risk insurance as lenders face growing exposure to AI infrastructure and data-center borrowers. The article says Barclays has sought coverage on more than USD 10 billion of data-center debt and that HSBC has explored a similar structure. It also reports that the Federal Reserve is preparing capital-rule changes and that Marsh expects an answer on the insurance treatment by year-end. These are media-reported statements and market examples, not a regulatory decision.

Credit insurance can transfer some borrower risk, but it does not make concentration risk disappear. Banks should test insurer credit quality, policy exclusions, attachment points, claims timing, counterparty concentration, and whether the coverage remains effective through a correlated AI infrastructure downturn. Regulators will ultimately care about real loss absorption and enforceable protection, not the label attached to the risk-transfer product.
