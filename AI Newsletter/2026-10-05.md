### Agentic Commerce Hits Inflection Point — and Nobody Wants to Own the Liability

**Source:** [FinTech Weekly](https://fintechweekly.com/magazine/articles/agentic-commerce-launch-month-liability-risk-2026)  •  **Date:** October 4, 2026

September 2026 was the busiest launch month yet for agentic commerce: Mastercard expanded Agent Pay with a scoring model estimating whether a transaction was AI-initiated; Meta's Muse launched with Stripe's Link checkout across more than one million businesses; Stripe made all hosted checkout pages agent-ready for 7.8 million merchants; and Shopify switched agent checkout on by default for eligible merchants. Six banks — including Bank of America, Capital One, ING, and NatWest — jointly published principles for "trusted agentic commerce." Despite the infrastructure sprint, actual adoption is minimal: 3% of transactions involve AI agents, and only 24% of US adults trust an agent to make routine purchases on their behalf. The liability gap is the live fault line: 93% of merchants believe AI providers should bear losses from erroneous purchases, yet no provider has accepted that responsibility. Amazon blocked Meta's Muse in September citing concerns about third-party applications acting on customer behalf.

The mismatch between merchant readiness (89%) and consumer trust (24%) is not a perception gap that marketing can close — it reflects a real absence of recourse frameworks. The six-bank principles signal that liability allocation is now a collective-action problem the industry must solve before agentic commerce can scale. Banks and issuers building payment products for AI agents should treat authorization evidence, intent verification, and dispute-trail completeness as non-negotiable before launch, not as post-launch compliance obligations.

### Bank of England FPC Elevates AI to Macroprudential Risk: 63% of Market Participants Now Flag It as Systemic

**Source:** [AI Commission](https://aicommission.org/2026/10/bank-of-england-warns-of-rising-financial-risks-from-energy-prices-and-ai/)  •  **Date:** October 2026

The Bank of England published its H2 2026 Systemic Risk Survey alongside the Financial Policy Committee record on September 30. The proportion of respondents citing AI as a source of risk rose 32 percentage points to 63%, making it the fastest-rising category and the third-most frequently cited systemic risk behind geopolitical risk (91%) and cyberattack (75%). The FPC identified four priority areas: greater AI use in core financial decision-making, greater AI use in financial markets, operational risks from AI service providers, and the evolving external cyber threat environment. The committee also flagged rapidly growing AI-related debt issuance as a contributing vulnerability. Governor Andrew Bailey's October speech framed the regulatory stance as testing-first: "understanding, testing and establishing credible points of intervention must come first," treating that as a prerequisite to formal regulation rather than an alternative to it.

The 32-percentage-point single-period jump is a significant signal shift. Until now, AI was framed primarily as a firm-level model risk and operational issue. The FPC's elevation to macroprudential status means regulators will increasingly look at correlated behavior, common provider dependencies, and synchronized market responses — not just individual bank AI controls. Chief risk officers should map shared AI model and infrastructure dependencies across their institution's ecosystem, not just within it, and stress-test for provider outages and correlated agent behavior.

The Promenaut relationship is a useful signal of what bank AI infrastructure actually requires in production. HSBC's decision to invest in and shape an orchestration layer, rather than build it internally or wait for a hyperscaler to provide it, reflects a real gap in available enterprise tooling. Banks assessing agentic deployment should treat orchestration governance — authorization, audit trail, escalation path — as the critical build-or-buy decision, not the model selection.

### Corgi Insurance Launches AI Infrastructure Coverage Programme, Including GPU Residual Value Protection

**Source:** [FinTech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/10/05/corgi-targets-growing-insurance-gap-around-ai-infrastructure/)  •  **Date:** October 5, 2026

Corgi Insurance launched a comprehensive coverage programme for AI infrastructure, spanning the construction, operational, and equipment phases of data center life cycles. The offering includes builders' risk insurance, delay-in-start-up coverage, operational infrastructure protection, and business income coverage. The distinctive addition is residual value protection for GPU hardware — treating GPU assets as financeable instruments rather than standard depreciating equipment. The programme targets colocation providers, GPU cloud businesses, AI compute hosts, and enterprise data center operators. Co-founder Nico Laqua stated that data centers are becoming critical global infrastructure but insurance has not evolved at the same pace.

GPU residual value protection is a product that makes sense only if insurers have a credible view of GPU depreciation curves, secondary-market liquidity, and obsolescence risk — all of which are highly uncertain in the current AI hardware cycle. Banks financing AI infrastructure (as Axis Bank signaled in its doubling of data center loans) face the same underwriting challenge. The emergence of a specialized insurer addressing this gap will matter both for direct insureds and for lenders who rely on collateral valuations. Banks building data center loan books should track whether this coverage product becomes a standard underwriting requirement.

### Refund Fraud Emerges as a Structural AML Blind Spot Requiring Unified FRAML Response

**Source:** [FinTech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/10/02/refund-fraud-is-exposing-a-new-aml-blind-spot/)  •  **Date:** October 2, 2026

Return fraud cost retailers over $101 billion in 2023, with approximately 14% of returns estimated as fraudulent. One US enforcement case involved $111 million in transactions routed through sham companies with manipulated chargeback ratios. Organized "refund-as-a-service" operations now coordinate claims across multiple accounts using Telegram and Discord, enabling fraud patterns that spread across merchants, payment instruments, and customer profiles simultaneously. The core AML problem is institutional: fraud and AML monitoring are typically managed in separate silos, making it difficult to detect velocity patterns when refund activity is distributed across multiple merchants. ZIGRAM advocates a unified "FRAML" approach — consolidating transaction, refund, chargeback, and behavioral data to enable entity resolution and velocity analysis across the full pattern.

The FRAML convergence argument has been made before, but refund fraud gives it operational urgency: the threat is not a sophisticated nation-state actor but highly organized criminal networks using commercially available platforms. Banks processing high-volume payment returns should audit whether their AML systems receive refund and chargeback data as inputs, or whether those signals are siloed in a separate fraud function. The warning signs flagged in the article — refunds to different payment instruments than original purchases, rapid transfer to crypto exchanges, multiple customer profiles sharing devices — are detectable with existing data if the systems are connected.
